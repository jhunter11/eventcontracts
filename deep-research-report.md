# Deep Research Assessment of an Event Contract Trading System for Kalshi and Polymarket

## Executive assessment

Your roadmap is directionally right, but the main practical conclusion from current platform documentation, fee schedules, and emerging market-structure research is that this project should be framed as **venue-specific execution research with selective alpha discovery**, not as a generic “prediction bot.” Kalshi and Polymarket are both binary-event venues, but they differ materially in matching architecture, collateral, settlement, resolution disputes, data access, rate limits, and incentive design. Those differences are large enough that a single backtester, a single fair-value model, or a single risk framework will give misleading results unless you normalize each venue separately first. citeturn32view0turn23view0turn22view0turn24view0turn24view1turn26search7turn34view1

The most promising initial edge classes are **Kalshi weather**, **Polymarket crypto-linked contracts**, **carefully normalized cross-venue divergences**, and **selected passive-liquidity strategies that explicitly include incentives and queue effects**. Sports and elections are still tradable research domains, but both now face more competition, more scrutiny, and more operational caveats than weather or crypto. On Polymarket, sports markets have venue-specific mechanics that automatically cancel outstanding limit orders at game start and impose a one-second placement delay on marketable orders, which is exactly the kind of hidden friction that destroys naïve backtests. citeturn40view1turn19view0turn36view1

The question is therefore not “Can prediction markets be forecast?” It is “Can you build an execution-aware research stack that measures whether a specific, mechanically grounded edge survives real venue rules, fees, latency, spread, queue position, resolution risk, and incentive distortions?” The available evidence says that some structural inefficiencies do exist, but they are not broad, free-floating mispricings. Recent research on Kalshi finds informative prices alongside favorite–longshot bias and better maker outcomes than taker outcomes; recent research on Polymarket finds measurable microstructure irregularities and shows that inferring trade direction from the public order-book feed is unreliable enough to contaminate many off-the-shelf alpha pipelines. citeturn16view0turn17view1turn17view2

## Repository implementation addendum

The repository now contains the first Python scaffold for the strategy/runner
contract described by this assessment. The scaffold is deliberately not a live
trading system. It establishes the typed object model and plug-in surface that
the later data, replay, paper execution, risk, gateway, and artifact layers can
share.

Current implementation highlights:

- `eventcontracts.domain` defines venue-neutral dataclasses for market data,
  orders, fills, positions, cash, lifecycle, settlement, features, strategy
  specs, sleeve specs, and typed IDs.
- `NormalizedEvent` is the strategy input surface. It covers quote, trade,
  order book, lifecycle, settlement, external signal, timer, own fill, own
  order update, and own order reject events.
- `StrategyDecision` is the strategy output surface. It covers place, cancel,
  replace, alert, and explicit no-action decisions.
- `StrategyContext` is a read-only protocol for positions, cash, exposure,
  open orders, features, and model predictions.
- `ExecutionPriority` and `LatencyTier` let strategies distinguish fast-decay
  edges, such as crypto movement, from slower prediction edges where being a
  second late may still be acceptable.
- `StrategyRunner` wires a strategy to event source, context provider, risk
  gate, intent sink, clock, and optional state store ports.
- `example_threshold` demonstrates the plug-in pattern end to end and the
  tests exercise registry resolution, runner dispatch, and risk rejection.

## Market structure facts that should reshape the plan

Kalshi is a **CFTC-regulated Designated Contract Market** that offers public REST market-data endpoints, authenticated WebSockets, FIX connectivity, historical endpoints, queue-position APIs, and a centralized event-contract matching environment. Its public REST market-data endpoints can be used without authentication, but its WebSocket connection requires authentication even for public market-data channels. Kalshi’s API now uses fixed-point prices and fractional contracts, and it has separated live and historical data, with a target live-data window of roughly three months before queries must be routed to historical endpoints. citeturn39search17turn32view0turn10view2turn22view3turn10view1

Polymarket’s global developer stack is structurally different. It is a **hybrid CLOB** with offchain matching and onchain settlement on Polygon; trades are collateralized in **pUSD**, a Polymarket ERC-20 token backed by USDC, and outcomes are tokenized through the Conditional Token Framework. Resolution uses the **UMA Optimistic Oracle**, with a disclosed challenge process that can extend resolution beyond the event end date. In other words, a Kalshi “contract” and a Polymarket “tokenized outcome” may look economically similar at the UI level while still carrying different settlement plumbing, dispute timing, and operational risk. citeturn19view2turn24view1turn24view3turn24view0

That difference matters directly for any cross-market arbitrage thesis. On Kalshi, the exchange automatically settles positions after determination and a settlement timer; the market lifecycle explicitly includes `determined`, `disputed`, `amended`, and `finalized` states. On Polymarket, outcome holders redeem through oracle- and adapter-driven token mechanics after resolution, and the challenge period is part of the process rather than a corner case. A “same question, different venue” spread is therefore not automatically a pure probability divergence; it may partly reflect collateral frictions, redemption timing, or dispute risk. That is an inference from the different lifecycle and resolution designs on the two venues. citeturn28view1turn28view0turn24view0turn24view2

A second major structural point is that “Polymarket” now needs to be disambiguated. The public Polygon-based venue documented in the global API docs is not the same thing as **Polymarket US**. CFTC filings from March 2026 show **QCX LLC d/b/a Polymarket US** operating as a designated contract market and separately certifying a liquidity-provider program and a market-incentive program. If your long-term goal is a regulated U.S. dual-venue system, you should treat Kalshi, global Polymarket, and Polymarket US as three distinct market structures rather than two brands. citeturn12view1turn12view2turn5search4

Infrastructure asymmetry also matters. Kalshi exposes rate-limit tiers that range from Basic to Prime, with explicit per-second read/write budgets, and offers FIX sessions plus private connectivity via AWS PrivateLink for lower-friction institutional connectivity. Polymarket exposes large public REST limits, throttles via Cloudflare rather than immediate rejection, and even documents direct co-location in `eu-west-2` for eligible participants. If your thesis is latency-based, server placement and network path are not implementation details; they are part of the strategy definition. citeturn10view0turn23view0turn34view0turn20view2

## Edge classes worth funding first

The cleanest first edge remains **Kalshi weather**. Kalshi’s weather markets settle to the **final NWS Daily Climate Report**, and the venue’s own help documentation explicitly warns that consumer weather apps do not determine outcomes and that local standard time conventions matter during daylight saving time. That is unusually favorable for systematic research, because it gives you a precise settlement source, objective resolution, and a tractable pipeline from external observations to contract fair value. You can pair Kalshi weather markets with the National Weather Service API, aviation weather/METAR feeds, and NOAA climate data services for point-in-time observational and historical data. citeturn40view1turn40view0turn6search0turn6search1turn6search4

This is also where an information-latency edge is most plausible. The critical question is not whether a forecast says 84°F or 86°F hours beforehand; it is whether **contract odds reprice with measurable lag when the last few degrees become far more or less likely** as fresh observations, METAR conditions, or updated forecast products arrive. Because weather contracts often hinge on threshold crossings, small late-day changes in observed or near-term forecast conditions can have large non-linear effects on fair value. Kalshi’s settlement-source specificity makes these fair-value updates measurable, which is exactly what a research-grade event study needs. citeturn40view1turn6search0turn6search1

The second strong domain is **crypto-linked event contracts**, especially on Polymarket’s global venue. Crypto trades around the clock, Polymarket itself trades against Polygon-based collateral and settlement infrastructure, and external reference data such as Binance mark prices and funding rates are machine-readable at one-second or three-second cadence. That makes crypto particularly suitable for lead-lag work, external-signal studies, and short-horizon movement prediction. The key point is not to predict terminal resolution first; it is to predict whether the next repricing wave is likely within the holding window you can actually monetize after costs. citeturn24view1turn6search2turn6search13turn5search19

The third edge class is **cross-venue divergence**, but only after contract normalization. The user-supplied plan is right to target Kalshi-versus-Polymarket spreads, yet in practice these spreads must be normalized for settlement wording, event end date, data source, dispute process, displayed-price conventions, and fee formulas. On Polymarket the displayed price is the midpoint unless the spread exceeds $0.10, in which case the last trade is shown; on Kalshi the order book publishes bids only, and asks must be inferred reciprocally from the opposite side. So the “same” 63% versus 71% spread can be a measurement artifact before it is a trading signal. citeturn25view0turn22view0turn22view2turn24view0turn40view0

The fourth promising class is **passive-liquidity provision in selected markets**, especially where incentives are material. Kalshi currently runs both a liquidity incentive program and a volume incentive program, and both may be changed or ended by the exchange; the liquidity program scores resting orders based on snapshots, size, and distance from the best price, while the volume program shares a reward pool based on trading volume in eligible markets. Polymarket’s maker economics are even more explicit: makers are not charged trading fees, certain markets offer maker rebates, and a liquidity-rewards program scores balanced quoting near the midpoint. This means passive-liquidity research should be framed as **spread capture plus rebates plus inventory risk plus adverse selection**, not just spread capture. citeturn41view0turn41view1turn12view0turn10view3

What should not be first. Sports already face venue-specific frictions and sharper competition. On Polymarket, sports markets cancel standing limit orders at game start and delay marketable orders by one second; on Kalshi, sports now also have dedicated live-data and play-by-play endpoints, which reduces the chance that rudimentary feed-scraping alone still creates a durable edge. Elections are still useful for low-frequency calibration and behavioral studies, but they now attract more public attention, political scrutiny, and better-informed flow than your first research cycle should target. citeturn40view1turn36view0turn36view1turn37search0turn38search0

## Data and replay architecture

A research-grade stack for this project should be built around **event-time replay**, not end-of-day history. On Kalshi, the minimum viable dataset is: market metadata, event metadata, series metadata including settlement sources, order-book snapshots or deltas, trade prints, market-lifecycle events, and the historical endpoints needed once data ages out of the live tier. You should store both exchange timestamps and local receipt timestamps, because Kalshi WebSocket trade updates already expose time fields such as `ts_ms`, and the venue’s historical/live partitioning means your backfill logic must be date-aware rather than endpoint-hardcoded. citeturn40view0turn22view1turn10view1turn28view1

On Polymarket, the equivalent minimum dataset is broader because of the venue’s hybrid design. You need Gamma API market and event discovery, CLOB order-book snapshots and WebSocket updates, price history, public trade history, market-resolution rules, and the onchain settlement layer that emits authoritative `OrderFilled` events. The reason is now supported by fresh empirical work: a 2026 Polymarket microstructure study found that trade direction inferred from the public order-book feed agreed with onchain ground truth in only about **59%** of buckets, far below the benchmark accuracy researchers often assume in equities. If you want serious microstructure features, join feed events to onchain fills rather than trusting feed-inferred aggressor side. citeturn33search3turn19view1turn33search2turn33search1turn5search12turn17view1

This also means your normalized schema should not just be “best bid, best ask, last trade.” It should separately preserve **quote state, trade state, resolution state, and incentive state**. Kalshi alone now has moving parts such as fixed-point prices, fractional contracts, series-level fee changes, incentive programs, settlement timers, market pauses, and queue-position observability. Polymarket adds negative-risk flags, minimum tick size, pUSD collateral handling, disputeable resolution, and matching-engine restarts. Schema versioning is therefore mandatory, especially because Polymarket’s CLOB V2 went live on April 28, 2026, and Kalshi changed pricing and size representations in April 2026 as well. citeturn22view3turn29view2turn39search2turn26search7turn31search6turn35search0

Your external-data layer should be narrow and high-quality at first. For weather, use NWS API observations and alerts, Aviation Weather METAR data, and NOAA climate services. For macro, use the BLS release calendar and API, FRED or ALFRED for historical vintages, and CME FedWatch probabilities for policy-rate contracts. For crypto, start with Binance mark price and funding-rate data before adding noisier onchain or social features. That combination is enough to test three of the strongest thesis classes—weather latency, macro-event repricing, and crypto lead-lag—without turning the project into a generic alternative-data warehouse. citeturn6search0turn6search1turn6search4turn6search3turn6search7turn8search1turn8search0turn8search3turn6search2turn6search13

A final data point from recent research sharpens the design choice: Kalshi research using transaction-level data through April 2025 found that contract prices were informative, accuracy improved as markets approached close, and makers outperformed takers, but the platform still exhibited favorite–longshot bias and relatively small volumes compared with institutional markets. That combination implies that **signal quality and execution quality are entangled**. You should therefore save every point-in-time order book, not just trades, because small markets can look highly informative at the close while remaining hard to monetize intraday. citeturn16view0

## Execution, simulation, and evaluation rules

The first rule is that **fees must be modeled at the order-fill level**, not as a flat haircut. Kalshi’s February 2026 fee schedule states the general taker-style fee as `round up(0.07 × C × P × (1-P))`, with separate maker-fee terms in applicable markets and rounding to the next cent. Polymarket’s fee formula is also non-linear: `fee = C × feeRate × p × (1-p)`, makers are not charged trading fees, and fee rates differ by market category. In both venues the cost burden is highest near 50% probability, which means a strategy that looks attractive on mid-price deviations around even odds is exactly where costs bite hardest. citeturn10view4turn29view0turn10view3

The second rule is that **displayed prices are not tradable prices**. On Polymarket, the displayed price is usually the midpoint but switches to last trade when the spread is wider than $0.10; on Kalshi, the API returns only bids, and asks must be derived from the complementary outcome side. Any backtest that uses UI price series as if they were executable will materially exaggerate both fill quality and cross-venue arbitrage frequency. The fair-value object in your simulator should therefore be a full local book, not a single probability number. citeturn25view0turn22view0turn22view2

The third rule is that **queue position matters most when you think you are being clever by posting passively**. Kalshi explicitly exposes queue-position endpoints and states that queue position is determined by price-time priority; its FIX documentation also notes that increasing the quantity of an existing order at the same point forfeits queue position. That is unusually useful because it lets you validate your passive-fill model against venue-native queue data instead of relying only on inferred book dynamics. On Polymarket, by contrast, you should assume queue must be reconstructed from local order-book state, order status, and fill sequence rather than taken from a dedicated queue-position API. citeturn30search0turn30search1turn30search2turn19view2

The fourth rule is that **maintenance, pauses, and venue restarts belong in the simulator**. Kalshi documents a weekly Thursday trading pause from 3:00 AM to 5:00 AM ET, with special handling for exchange pauses and optional `CancelOrderOnPause` behavior. Polymarket documents matching-engine restarts and a post-restart operational regime. A backtest that assumes uninterrupted trading around venue maintenance windows will systematically mis-estimate maker fill persistence and inventory risk. citeturn28view2turn23view0turn35search0

The fifth rule is that **resolution risk is part of PnL timing**. On Kalshi, markets settle after determination and a settlement timer, and results can be disputed or amended before finalization. On Polymarket, a proposal can be disputed during a two-hour challenge window and can escalate further. Research that scores strategies only on marked-to-mid PnL but ignores time-to-redemption and dispute extensions will overstate capital efficiency, especially for cross-venue convergence trades and event-resolution mispricing strategies. citeturn28view1turn28view0turn24view0turn24view2

That leads to the correct evaluation focus. For this domain, the most useful research metrics are not raw return and win rate. They are **expected value per filled trade after venue fees, slippage relative to local-book estimates, realized fill rate versus simulated fill rate, calibration by market category, and edge decay as a function of latency bucket**. The Kalshi evidence on maker-versus-taker outcomes and the Polymarket evidence on feed-versus-ground-truth trade direction both point to the same lesson: execution realism is not downstream from alpha; it is a primary determinant of whether the alpha exists for you at all. citeturn16view0turn17view1

## Compliance, venue access, and adversarial risk

A deployment plan that ignores compliance will not survive contact with these venues. Prediction-market regulation is still active policy terrain: the CFTC launched an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on prediction markets in March 2026, and in May 2026 it sued Minnesota to block a state law banning prediction markets, explicitly framing federal jurisdiction as live. That does not mean the platforms are unsafe; it means product scope, permitted contracts, and venue access should be treated as dynamic system inputs rather than fixed assumptions. citeturn37search0turn38search0turn38search2

Geography is part of that. Polymarket’s official geoblock documentation states that order placement is restricted in certain countries and regions, includes the United States on the blocked-country list for the documented global venue, and exposes an API endpoint to check geographic eligibility before placing orders. Kalshi separately states that account eligibility is subject to country restrictions and member-agreement terms. A dual-venue trading system therefore needs a **hard pre-trade policy service** that knows which venue, account, region, and market category are permitted before an order ticket is even constructed. citeturn20view2turn20view3turn9search0turn9search2

The insider-trading issue is no longer theoretical. In February 2026 the CFTC’s Division of Enforcement issued a prediction-markets advisory tied to Kalshi enforcement matters involving misuse of nonpublic information and direct influence over contract outcomes. In March 2026 the CFTC’s enforcement director publicly stated that insider trading law applies in prediction markets and highlighted exchange responsibilities as the “first lines of defense.” In April 2026 the DOJ announced charges against a U.S. soldier who allegedly made more than $400,000 trading Polymarket based on classified information about a military operation. Any serious system should simply exclude strategy classes that depend on privileged or ethically suspect information channels. citeturn15view0turn15view1turn15view2

Adversarial microstructure research is also justified. The recent Polymarket microstructure paper reports a median self-counterparty wash share of about 1% with an upper tail reaching 22% in its panel, while official venue programs on both Kalshi and Polymarket explicitly discuss incentives for market making, trading activity, and anti-abuse monitoring. That does not imply the venues are dominated by manipulation, but it does imply that your surveillance layer should include self-trade filters, sudden-book-withdrawal features, abnormal quote-to-fill ratios, and incentive-period regime tags before you trust any behavioral signal extracted from raw order flow. citeturn17view1turn12view0turn41view0turn41view1

## Ninety-day build sequence

In the first month, build the **canonical market-data lake and replay layer**. For Kalshi that means public REST market discovery, order books, trade feed ingestion, lifecycle events, series settlement sources, and automatic routing to historical endpoints after the live-data cutoff. For Polymarket that means Gamma event discovery, CLOB order books and market WebSocket capture, price history, Data API trades, resolution rules, and an authoritative onchain fill pipeline for later trade-direction joins. At the same time, stand up a narrow external-data layer: NWS plus METAR for weather, BLS plus FRED plus CME FedWatch for macro, and Binance mark/funding for crypto. citeturn32view0turn10view1turn40view0turn19view1turn33search1turn33search2turn24view0turn6search0turn6search1turn6search3turn8search1turn8search0turn6search2

In the second month, run **three tightly scoped research programs** rather than ten loose ones. The first should be a weather event-study engine on Kalshi: how fast do threshold probabilities move after new observations or forecast updates, and how does fillability change in the final hours? The second should be a Polymarket crypto lead-lag engine: do mark price, funding, or volatility shocks predict short-horizon price changes net of taker fees? The third should be a normalization engine for cross-venue spreads that rejects mismatched contracts and records why they were rejected, because that rejection log will teach you almost as much as the accepted sample. citeturn40view1turn6search2turn6search13turn25view0turn22view0turn24view0

In the third month, move into **paper execution with full venue realism**. On Kalshi, use the queue-position APIs, native maker/taker costs, Thursday pause handling, and settlement-timer logic. On Polymarket, use category-specific taker fees, post-only handling, sports-specific cancellation rules where relevant, and order-book-walk slippage estimates rather than midpoint assumptions. Run walk-forward tests by contract family, not pooled across everything, because edge persistence is almost certainly category-specific. citeturn30search0turn30search1turn10view4turn28view2turn10view3turn31search7turn40view1

The go/no-go threshold for live deployment should be strict. You should not deploy because the model forecasts outcomes well. You should deploy only if at least one strategy family shows positive expectancy **after** venue fees, simulated slippage, realistic latency, and paper-trading decay; if realized paper fills are close enough to simulated fills to trust the execution model; and if the strategy does not rely on ambiguous resolution language, blocked geographies, or information channels that create legal or reputational hazard. That standard is exactly what separates quantitative event-contract research from sophisticated gambling.
